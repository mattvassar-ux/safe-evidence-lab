Witnessing-Based ECHO

Testimony-Driven Training to Prevent MOUD Discontinuation During Insurance Disruption

For a patient taking buprenorphine, losing insurance is not a paperwork problem. Withdrawal can begin within a day of a missed dose. This project records the experiences of Oklahomans in recovery who lost or changed coverage and turns them into Project ECHO teaching sessions, so clinicians learn to spot coverage risk and act before treatment stops.

The problem

A coverage gap can become a crisis in a day.

About two-thirds of patients stop buprenorphine within six months, and insurance type is one of the most consistent predictors of who stops early. When Medicaid eligibility lapses or a job ends, the window between a missed dose and withdrawal is short, and the risk of return to use and overdose rises with it.

Many Oklahoma patients receive medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD) from primary care and behavioral health clinicians, often in rural areas. Few of those clinicians are trained to treat insurance instability as a clinical risk: to ask about coverage, recognize when it is about to change, and connect patients to help in time.

What's new

Patient testimony as the teaching case

Project ECHO trains clinicians through short, case-based video sessions with a specialist team. ECHO cases are usually written vignettes. This project replaces them with the recorded words of people who lived through coverage loss: the phone calls, the gaps, and what they did not tell their prescriber for fear of losing access.

The approach draws on the lab's witnessing methodology, which treats testimony as evidence to be received with care rather than an anecdote to illustrate a point. Participants choose, through a tiered consent, how their words may be used, from a paraphrase read by someone else to their own recorded voice.

How it works

Three phases

  • 01 · Listen

    Testimony interviews

    Trauma-informed interviews with 20 to 30 uninsured or underinsured Oklahomans in recovery about coverage history, enrollment barriers, and where care broke down. Recordings are transcribed and de-identified.

  • 02 · Build

    Six teaching cases

    Interviews are coded and shaped into six cases, each with decision points: the question that could have been asked, the referral that was not made. An advisory panel reviews every case before use.

  • 03 · Teach

    Six ECHO sessions

    Monthly one-hour sessions for 30 to 40 clinicians: a short lesson on insurance navigation, a testimony-based case, and group discussion. Sessions are recorded, and CME credit is offered.

Where we are

Timeline

We will measure clinicians' confidence in insurance navigation before and after the series, and ask at 30 and 90 days whether they now check coverage, refer patients to navigators, or adjust treatment plans when coverage is at risk.

  1. Fall 2026

    Protocol and ethics review

    Interview guide, tiered consent, and study protocol prepared for institutional review board submission.

  2. Winter 2026–27

    Interviews

    Recruitment and testimony interviews, followed by transcription and de-identification.

  3. Spring 2027

    Case development

    Qualitative analysis, excerpt selection, and advisory panel review of the six teaching cases.

  4. 2027

    ECHO series and results

    Monthly ECHO sessions, follow-up surveys, and an open-access paper. The teaching cases will remain available for medical student education.
