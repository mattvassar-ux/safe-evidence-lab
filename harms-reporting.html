Improving the Integrity of Harms Reporting

Clinical trials record harms in public registries, but the published papers that doctors and guideline panels read often report fewer of them, or describe them in softer language. This project builds validated measures of that gap, tests whether it changes what happens downstream, and pilots tools that help journals catch it before publication.

The problem

The registry says one thing. The paper says another.

Across 16 registry-to-publication studies led by our medical student teams, the same pattern appeared in nearly every specialty we examined.

  • —

    Missing counts

    Publications under-reported deaths and serious adverse events compared with the trial's own registry entry.

  • —

    Missing dropouts

    Discontinuations due to adverse events appeared in registries but rarely in the journal article.

  • —

    Unsupported reassurance

    Some papers stated that "no serious adverse events occurred" when the registry recorded them.

  • —

    Softened language

    Phrases like "well tolerated," "mild," and "transient" framed harms as smaller than the numbers showed.

Two new measures

Scoring what a trial reports, and how it says it

Each trial is scored from its paired ClinicalTrials.gov record and journal article. Both indices run from 0 to 1 and can be compared across specialties, sponsors, and years.

HRI

Harm Reporting Integrity Index

Higher is better. Measures how completely and accurately harms are reported.

  • CompletenessAre serious adverse events, deaths, the safety population, the time at risk, and discontinuations due to harm all reported?
  • ConcordanceDo the paper's counts of serious events, deaths, and discontinuations match the registry?
  • TransparencyAre the safety population, the definition of a serious event, and the reporting window stated clearly?
  • TimelinessHow long after the trial ends do harms appear in print? Full credit within 36 months.

FRI

Framing Risk Index

Lower is better. Measures language that downplays or obscures harm.

  • Unsupported absence claims"No serious adverse events occurred" when the registry shows at least one.
  • Positivity over burden"Well tolerated" in trials with meaningful rates of serious harm.
  • Comparator maskingRelative risk only ("50% fewer events") with no absolute counts.
  • Hedging intensityThe share of harms sentences softened by words like "mild," "transient," or "rarely."
  • Selective specificityMinor events itemized in detail while serious ones are lumped together.

Specific aims

Measure, explain, intervene

  1. Aim 1 · Measure

    Build and validate the indices

    Assemble a large corpus of paired registry records and publications for phase II–IV adult trials completed 2010–2023. Develop the HRI and FRI against a gold-standard human annotation, with inter-rater reliability of at least 0.70, then automate scoring with natural language processing.

  2. Aim 2 · Explain

    Trace the downstream consequences

    Test whether reporting quality predicts what happens next: FDA safety label changes, uptake into clinical guidelines, citation influence, and investigator careers. Model whether sponsor practices, journal checklists, and data-sharing norms act through reporting quality.

  3. Aim 3 · Intervene

    Pilot tools for journals

    Co-design interventions with journal editors, reviewers, and regulators: an automated registry–publication discrepancy flag, an editorial checklist, and framing prompts. Test them retrospectively and simulate their effect on reporting quality.
