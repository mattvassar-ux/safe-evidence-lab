Computational Meta-Research Training

An eight-week summer institute that teaches biomedical students to audit clinical trial evidence the way computational scientists work: querying registries through APIs, running reproducible checks in the cloud, and keeping every decision in version control.

Why it matters

Hand-checking trials can't keep up with the record.

ClinicalTrials.gov alone holds more than 400,000 studies. For over a decade our students have audited them by hand, and that work has produced hundreds of papers on unpublished results, switched outcomes, and missing harms. But one student can check only 50 to 100 trials in a summer, and two careful reviewers often disagree with no record of why.

The bottleneck is no longer understanding why transparency matters. It is computational skill. This pilot nests a cloud-enabled training track inside our existing summer program so students learn to audit thousands of trials reproducibly, and can keep doing it at home institutions without their own computing clusters.

What trainees learn

Five competencies, taught through real trial data

Students don't learn APIs or Docker in the abstract. They learn them by auditing actual trials, from the first query to a finished, reproducible capstone.

  • 01 · Data access

    Distributed data access

    Query trial registries programmatically, handle pagination and rate limits, and combine records from several sources.

  • 02 · Reproducibility

    Reproducible workflows

    Build containerized environments, track work with Git and GitHub, and document analyses in notebooks.

  • 03 · Validation

    Automated validation

    Run structured validators, read their outputs critically, and trace every flag back to its source evidence.

  • 04 · Appraisal

    Evidence evaluation

    Apply reporting standards such as CONSORT, ICMJE, and FDAAA to recognize where trial reporting falls short.

  • 05 · Collaboration

    Collaborative science

    Review each other's code, share work through open repositories, and communicate technical findings clearly.

The summer institute

Eight weeks, one capstone audit

A short self-paced primer in the shell, Git, and Python or R comes first. No prior programming experience is required.

  1. Week 1

    Meta-research and the ClinicalTrials.gov API

    Computational thinking and your first programmatic queries.

  2. Week 2

    Version control and notebooks

    Git, GitHub, and reproducible Jupyter workflows.

  3. Week 3

    Containers and validators

    Docker, then running FAIROTrials validators on sample records.

  4. Week 4

    Registry–publication comparison

    Edge cases, false positives, and findings that need a human call.

  5. Week 5

    Working at scale

    Batch processing, parallel workflows, and evidence synthesis.

  6. Weeks 6–7

    Capstone

    An independent computational audit, refined through peer code review.

  7. Week 8

    Presentations

    Capstone talks and final skills assessment.

Open tools

A cloud lab and the FAIROTrials validators

Trainees work in a browser-based Jupyter environment in the cloud, so nothing has to be installed and no local computing cluster is needed. The same workflows also run on OSU's High Performance Computing Center or a personal laptop.

Seven validators check

  • —

    FAIR metadata and identifiers

  • —

    FDAAA results-reporting timeliness

  • —

    Link accessibility

  • —

    Registered versus published primary outcomes

  • —

    CONSORT-Harms completeness

  • —

    Registry–publication linkage

  • —

    Data-sharing transparency

Validators are screening aids. A flag prompts a closer look at the evidence; it is not a verdict. Advanced trainees can propose improvements and submit pull requests, which are merged only after mentor review.

Who it's for

Two cohorts of biomedical learners

Medical students, graduate students in public health, epidemiology, and biostatistics, pharmacy and nursing doctoral students, and early-career clinical researchers. We especially welcome learners from institutions without local computing resources, including rural and regional campuses and EPSCoR states.
